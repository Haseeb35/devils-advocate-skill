# With vs without the skill: opening a second coffee shop

The same decision, asked two ways in Claude Code with the same model. Both outputs are unedited.

## Without the skill

> **Prompt:** I'm thinking of opening a second location of my coffee shop across town. the first one has been profitable for 2 years, I'd take a bank loan for most of it. what do you think?

Two profitable years is a solid start, but it doesn't settle the question on its own. A second location is a different business problem from the first, and a bank loan raises the stakes. Here's how I'd think about it.

## What works in your favor
- **Your concept is proven.** You know your margins, costs and what customers want.
- **Banks like an operating history.** Two profitable years makes the loan easier to get and may lower the rate.
- **Some costs can be shared.** Supplier pricing, roasting or baking, marketing and admin can cover two shops.

## Where second locations usually go wrong
1. **You can't be in both places.** If the first shop runs well because you're there every day, the hard part is a manager you trust, not the new building. Ask yourself whether shop #1 could run for a month without you. If it couldn't, fix that first.
2. **Across town is a different market.** Your current profit comes from your current foot traffic and regulars, and they won't follow you. Treat the new site like a fresh launch. Count foot traffic at different times of day, check the competition, and see who's actually nearby (offices, students, homes).
3. **The ramp-up takes cash.** New shops often lose money for 6 to 18 months while they build regulars. Your loan payment starts right away anyway. If shop #1's profit has to cover shop #2's losses plus the debt, both shops are at risk.
4. **The loan is tied to you.** Small business loans often need a personal guarantee or use the first shop as collateral. If the second shop fails, it can take the first one down with it.

## Numbers to work out before deciding
- **How much shop #1 really earns after paying yourself a fair salary.** That's your real cushion.
- **Break-even for shop #2.** Work out how many cups a day it needs to sell to cover rent, staff, the loan payment and cost of goods. Then ask whether that's realistic for that street.
- **A bad-case test.** If shop #2 makes 60% of your forecast for its first year, can you still make every loan payment? If not, the plan is too tight.
- **Reserves outside the loan.** Have 4 to 6 months of operating costs for the new shop set aside, separate from the build-out budget.

## My take
It can be a good move, but only if these three things hold:
- Shop #1 runs without you.
- The new site checks out on its own merits.
- You can survive a slow first year without putting shop #1 at risk.

If any of them is shaky, there are cheaper ways to test the market first. You could run a kiosk, a cart, a pop-up, or a wholesale deal with a nearby office or café. Another option is a smaller loan with more of your own money in it.

If you share rough figures, I can build a simple break-even and loan model for you. The useful ones are shop #1's monthly revenue and profit, the expected rent and build-out cost for the new site, and the loan amount and terms.

## With the skill

> **Prompt:** devil's advocate: I'm thinking of opening a second location of my coffee shop across town. the first one has been profitable for 2 years, I'd take a bank loan for most of it

### 😈 Devil's Advocate

**The plan:** Open a second coffee shop across town and pay for most of it with a bank loan, on the strength of two profitable years at the first shop. I'm assuming you run the first shop day to day, and that the loan will probably need a personal guarantee.

**The case against**
Two years of profit shows that *this* shop works at *this* address. That may be thanks to you being behind the counter, a regular crowd that built up over time, and a spot you know well. A second shop takes away the first two: you can't be in both places, and the new crowd starts at zero. Meanwhile the loan payments start in month one, whether or not people are walking through the door. A personal guarantee also puts more than the business at risk: the first shop's profits, and possibly your home, end up backing the second one's losses.

**3 ways this could fail**
1. **The new shop takes longer than your cash lasts.** Most cafés need 6–18 months to build a steady crowd. If you planned around the first shop's current sales, you'll run out of money before you get there.
   Likelihood: High · Warning sign: sales are under about 60% of plan by month 4 · Prevent it: plan on reaching break-even in 12+ months, and borrow or set aside 6 months of running costs plus loan payments on top of build-out costs.
2. **The first shop slips while you're away.** Your attention moves to the new site, and quality, staff morale and regulars drift at the shop that pays the bills, right when the second one needs that cash.
   Likelihood: Medium–High · Warning sign: weekly sales or reviews at shop #1 drop while you're busy at #2 · Prevent it: hire and train a manager at shop #1 *before* you sign the lease, and test it by taking two weeks fully off.
3. **The new location is wrong in ways you can't see yet.** Across town can mean different foot traffic, a different mix of customers, more competition, or harder parking. Build-out also usually runs 20–40% over budget.
   Likelihood: Medium · Warning sign: you picked the site because the rent was cheap, not because you watched the traffic · Prevent it: count foot traffic at the site at 7–9am on several weekdays, check nearby competitors, and add a 25% contingency to the build-out budget.

**The case for**
Two years of profit is real, and it's more than most new cafés can show. You already have suppliers, recipes, a system for training staff and a brand people know. Some costs can be shared between shops, like buying in bulk, roasting and admin. If the first shop runs well without you, a second one is the normal way to grow, and the loan lets you do it without giving up ownership.

**Verdict: ⚠️ Go, with changes**
The idea is sound, but don't sign until three things are true:
- The first shop runs without you for a month.
- The loan plus your reserves cover about 12 months of slow sales at the new shop.
- You've checked the site with your own eyes at peak hours.

Also try to limit or negotiate the personal guarantee. Have an accountant look over the loan terms, because that's where most of the downside sits.

**Before you decide:** If the second shop only broke even for 18 months, could the first shop's profits cover the loan payments without putting your home or savings at risk?
