## 😈 Devil's Advocate (Deep Mode)

**The plan:** Quit your job and build a SaaS for dental clinics full time. You have 8 months of savings and 3 clinics who said they'd try it. *Assumptions:* you're a solo founder, there's no product or revenue yet, and none of the 3 clinics has paid or signed anything.

**The case against**
"We'd try it" is the cheapest yes a clinic can give. It costs them nothing and commits them to nothing. You'd be giving up a steady income on the strength of three polite yeses. Dental clinics are also a hard market: they already run practice-management software that holds their records, billing and schedule. Staff are busy, switching is painful, and sales cycles are slow. Eight months sounds like a lot. Once you subtract the build, onboarding and the slow sales cycle, it's very little time to reach revenue that covers your living costs.

**Hidden assumptions**
1. ⚠️ **"Would try it" will turn into "will pay for it."** This is the shakiest one. Nothing so far tests whether they'll pay.
2. The problem you're solving hurts enough for a clinic to change how its front desk works, not just "nice to have."
3. You can integrate with, or sit alongside, the software clinics already use without a long fight over integrations or data access.
4. Three clinics represent a wider market. They're not friends, past colleagues, or favors.
5. Eight months of savings really means eight months. Your number already covers taxes, health cover, software and tool costs, and a buffer.

**5 ways this could fail** (ranked by likelihood × damage)

1. **The pilots never convert to paying customers.** The clinics use it for free, say "this is great," and stall when you send an invoice. Or they quietly stop logging in.
   Likelihood: **High** · Warning sign: they won't agree to a price or start date before you build · Prevent it: get a paid pilot or a signed letter of intent with a price *before* you quit. Even a small amount counts.

2. **The runway runs out before revenue arrives.** The build takes 3–4 months, onboarding takes another 1–2, and a slow sales cycle uses up the rest. You hit month 6 with $500 MRR and panic.
   Likelihood: **High** · Warning sign: no paying customer by month 3 · Prevent it: work backwards from a set date to go back to a job. Plan a launch at weeks 6–8 with a narrow MVP, not a full platform.

3. **You build too much product for the wrong problem.** You spend months on scheduling, billing, charting and reminders, and compete with incumbents on every front. Meanwhile the clinics only cared about one thing.
   Likelihood: **Medium–High** · Warning sign: your feature list keeps growing and the clinics give you vague feedback · Prevent it: find the *one* painful workflow (no-shows, recalls, insurance follow-up, reviews…) and win on that alone.

4. **Integration and compliance get in the way.** Patient data brings privacy rules (for example HIPAA in the US, or your local equivalent). Practice-management vendors may also lock down their APIs. Either one can add months, or make clinics' IT people say no.
   Likelihood: **Medium** · Warning sign: the clinics ask "does it connect to our system?" and you don't have an answer · Prevent it: confirm what data you'll touch and how you'll get it. Talk to a compliance expert for one hour before writing code.

5. **Sales becomes the real job, and you didn't plan for it.** Even if 3 clinics convert, you need 30–100 to replace your salary. Selling to dentists clinic by clinic is grinding, in-person work.
   Likelihood: **Medium** · Warning sign: you can't name your channel for clinics 4–20 (a dental association, a supplier partnership, a consultant network…) · Prevent it: write down your plan for reaching your next 20 customers now, and test it with 10 cold outreaches.

**Pre-mortem**
*It's November 2027, and you're back in a full-time job.* Here's what happened. You quit in December and spent the first four months building a scheduling and reminders platform you were proud of. You onboarded the three clinics in April. One was a friend's practice and used it lightly. The second had a receptionist who never stopped using their old system. The third liked it but said they'd "budget for it next quarter." By June you had one customer paying $150 a month. You spent July and August cold-calling clinics and learned that most were locked into multi-year contracts with their practice-management vendor. In September, with six weeks of savings left, you started interviewing. The product wasn't bad. You had found out too late whether anyone would pay for it, and you had no time left to change course.

**Kill criteria**
- **No signed or paying customer by the end of month 3** → stop building features and do nothing but sales and discovery for 4 weeks. If you still have none, start job hunting.
- **Fewer than 2 of the 3 pilots actively using it weekly by month 4** → the problem isn't painful enough. Change what you're solving or stop.
- **Under about 30% of your monthly costs covered by MRR at month 6** → go back to a job or contract work, and keep the product going part time.

**The case for**
Vertical SaaS for clinics can be an excellent business. Customers stick around once you're embedded, they pay reliably, and dentistry is full of manual workflows that existing tools handle badly. Having 3 clinics willing to talk to you is a real start that most founders don't have. If you're early in your career with low fixed costs, the downside is a year's delay plus skills that employers value. That's a survivable bet.

**Verdict: ⚠️ Go, with changes**
The idea isn't the problem. The order of steps is. Before you resign: (1) turn at least one of the 3 clinics into a paid commitment, even a small one; (2) narrow the product to the single workflow they'd pay for; (3) settle your real burn rate and a date to go back to work. If you can do those while still employed, quit with confidence. If the clinics won't commit to any price, you've learned that for free.

**Before you decide:** If you asked each of the 3 clinics for a payment today to reserve a spot, how many would say yes?

It's your life and your call. Since this involves your finances (and patient-data compliance), a quick conversation with a financial advisor, and later with a healthcare-compliance specialist, is worth it.
